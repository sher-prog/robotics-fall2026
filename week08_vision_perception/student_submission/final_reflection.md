What stood out to me most was how easily the robot's vision can be fooled. My color detector picked the shadowed wood instead of the mug, because they were almost the same color. Even the learned detector missed the mug in a lot of the images. Before this lab I thought a camera plus a good program would just work, and now I know it depends a lot on the conditions. I also liked seeing how one setting changes behavior. When I raised the steering gain from 0.08 to 1.0, the robot followed the track much more smoothly. It was satisfying to change one thing and see the result in the plots and in the simulation. This made me more interested in the perception side of robotics, because it's where things go wrong in surprising ways. It also made me think about people. A robot that is confident but wrong could be a real problem around people, so safe stops, like the robot stopping when it lost the line, matter as much as the detection itself. The setup was frustrating at times. I had some build and update problems before I could start, and I had to ask the professor for help. But solving them taught me to read error messages and keep backups of my work. Overall, I'm more motivated to keep doing this kind of work, and I now check results before trusting them.
